The strikeout rate barely moves — 7.57 per nine in relief against 5.55 starting — so this is not a story about losing stuff. It is a story about command and damage. He walks 3.6× as many hitters per nine as a starter and gives up 15.3× as many home runs.

The dates matter. His last start was 2026-06-04; his first relief appearance was 2026-05-05. He has not started since, and the bullpen line has been accumulating ever since.

What this is not. 54.7 innings across 12 appearances is 4.6 innings a time — this is bulk relief, not a seventh-inning specialist, so the comparison is closer to like-for-like than a closer's ERA would be. It is still 35.7 innings of starting, which is a handful of bad afternoons away from looking ordinary. Both samples are small and the gap is enormous; the honest reading is that the gap is far too large to be entirely real, and far too large to be entirely noise either.

It also caught this site out once. The bullpen availability table used to read roles off the roster, where MLB tags every pitcher on the 26-man as a starter, and Bello is exactly the case that exposed it — a man who opened the season in the rotation and has relieved exclusively since midsummer. The table now reads role from the last thirty days of usage instead.

The window is not what it looks like. A "last ten games" split on a part-time catcher is not a fortnight — his was 52 days wide, because he did not appear at all between then and 2026-08-17. Read as recent form it looks like a hot fortnight. It is really a player who went away and came back different, and the two are not the same claim.

The strikeout rate is the part that argues for something real. He struck out in 18% of plate appearances before the break and 3% since — power surges are common, power surges accompanied by a collapsing strikeout rate are less so. His home runs came on 2026-08-17, 2026-08-18, 2026-08-19, 2026-08-25.

And 24 at-bats is 24 at-bats. Put a number on it: if he were a league-average power hitter — about 3.5% of at-bats ending in a home run — the chance of 5 or more in 24 is 0.0013, roughly one in 783. That is small enough to say the rate has genuinely changed and far too small a sample to say what it changed to. A .476 average will not survive contact with September. The question worth watching is not whether he keeps slugging 1.167 — he will not — but whether the strikeout rate stays down when the batted-ball luck stops.
